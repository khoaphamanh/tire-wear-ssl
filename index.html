Estimating tire wear from operating signals with self-supervised pretraining

TL;DR
Problem
In industry, machines record far more data than anyone can label: unlabeled data is abundant, labeled data is scarce and expensive.
Question
How can the unlabeled data improve supervised learning (SL) when only a few labeled samples exist?
Answer
Self-supervised learning (SSL): pretrain a model on the unlabeled data, then fine-tune it on the few labels.
Result
On tire-wear estimation for a vehicle test platform, SSL cuts the error by 54 % compared with the best conventional SL method trained on the labeled data alone (0.28 mm vs 0.60 mm mean absolute error).

01 · Motivation

A test platform that wears its own tires

Before a new car model goes into production, its driver-assistance systems are tested on the road against a realistic target: a soft dummy car carried by the UFO (ultra-flat overrunable) platform, a driverless robot that the test vehicle can run over without damage.

The platform drives on small solid rubber tires. Hard braking and steering wear flat spots into them, which degrade its driving behavior and shorten its service life. Today the wear is known only from manual measurements between test campaigns: the tires come off, are scanned, and the platform stands still. Meanwhile, it records its sensor signals in every single run.

The goal: estimate tire wear from those recordings, so that tires are replaced when their condition requires it instead of on a fixed schedule.

1

No wear sensor

A flat spot shows up only indirectly in the signals, buried under the much larger variation from trajectory, speed and road surface.

2

Few labels

Every label means taking the tires off and scanning them. Only 190 runs carry a wear label, while more than a year of operating data carries none.

3

Unseen conditions

The model has to estimate wear on a driving maneuver for which no labeled run exists at all.

02 · Data

Sensor time series, few labels

Input

Multivariate time series sampled at 100 Hz with 3 features: vertical acceleration and the rotational speed of both front wheels.

Windowing

Each run is cut into 5 s windows (500 time steps). The model predicts wear per window; the prediction for a run is the mean over its windows.

Label

Tire wear in mm, one value per run: the depth of the flat spot, taken from the more worn of the two front tires.

A deliberately hard split

Two driving maneuvers: SystemCheck (SC), a slow check run at about 25 km/h, and LaneChange (LC), a lane change at about 50 km/h. Training uses labeled SC runs only. The test split adds LC, so every model has to transfer to a maneuver it has never seen with a label, at wear levels that do not occur in training.

03 · Methods

Two ways to learn: self-supervised or supervised

SSL first learns a representation from the unlabeled data, then fine-tunes it on the few labels. SL learns from the labeled data only, and the unlabeled data stays unused.

06 · Findings

What the experiments show

−54 % error

Unlabeled data pays off

The best pretrained model reaches 0.28 mm against 0.60 mm for the best supervised method, with the same labeled data, split and evaluation.

0.37 vs 0.70 mm on LC

The gain is largest where labels end

Pretrained models estimate wear on the LaneChange maneuver without a single labeled LC run. No supervised baseline improves noticeably on a constant guess there.

0.28 vs 0.42 mm

Relevant data beats more data

Pretraining on unlabeled recordings of the two target maneuvers beat pretraining on all 45 maneuver types the platform drives.

Scope. Small encoders, a short pretraining budget on one GPU, and a test split of 90 runs: differences of a few hundredths of a millimeter lie within its noise.
